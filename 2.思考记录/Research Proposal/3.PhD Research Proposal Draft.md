# Towards Context-Adaptive Spatial Analysis: Evaluating and Selecting Spatial Neighbourhood Representations Across Diverse Urban Morphologies

## 1. Abstract

Urban spatial analysis frequently relies on predefined spatial representations, such as Euclidean buffers, network-based catchments and fixed spatial units. However, the suitability of these methods may vary across cities with different morphological characteristics, network configurations and physical barriers. As urban environments evolve, conventional spatial representations may not consistently reflect the spatial processes being investigated.

This research aims to systematically evaluate the suitability of alternative spatial neighbourhood representations across different urban contexts and investigate how methodological choices influence spatial measurements and statistical inference. Drawing on the Uncertain Geographic Context Problem (UGCoP), the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) and uncertainty propagation, the study will combine comparative GIS experiments, urban morphological analysis, Monte Carlo simulations and spatial statistical methods.

The expected contribution is an interpretable, context-adaptive framework for evaluating and selecting spatial neighbourhood representations. Rather than assuming that more complex methods are always preferable, the research seeks to identify the conditions under which simpler representations remain analytically adequate and when more detailed approaches are necessary.

## 2. Research Background

Urban environments are characterised by substantial spatial heterogeneity and continuous structural change. Differences in street connectivity, block configuration, transport infrastructure, land-use distribution and physical barriers influence the organisation of urban space and the accessibility of locations. Urban expansion, infrastructure development and redevelopment may further modify these spatial relationships over time.

Geographical Information Systems (GIS) provide methods for representing and analysing these complex urban environments. Before conducting spatial measurements or statistical analysis, researchers often need to define geographical neighbourhoods or analytical units. Common approaches include Euclidean buffers, network-based catchments, fixed-distance thresholds and regular spatial grids. These methods are widely adopted because they offer standardised and reproducible procedures for organising spatial data.

However, each method relies on particular assumptions about spatial relationships. For example, a circular buffer represents proximity through straight-line distance, whereas a network-based catchment considers movement through connected routes. In a highly connected pedestrian network, the differences between these representations may be relatively limited. In an urban area divided by rivers, railways, motorways or large impermeable blocks, the differences may be substantially greater.

More importantly, differences in spatial representation do not necessarily produce equivalent differences in analytical results. Two neighbourhood representations may have significantly different spatial boundaries but generate similar measurements if the relevant urban characteristics are distributed relatively uniformly. Conversely, relatively small changes in neighbourhood boundaries may include or exclude important concentrations of facilities or activities, thereby altering measurements and subsequent statistical estimates.

This raises a broader methodological question: **how should spatial analytical methods be evaluated and selected in relation to the characteristics of the urban environment and the purpose of the analysis?**

Existing research demonstrates that neighbourhood delineation, spatial aggregation and data-processing decisions can influence geographical analysis. Nevertheless, selecting a method remains challenging because spatial complexity does not automatically indicate the magnitude of its analytical consequences.

There is therefore a need to examine not only how alternative spatial methods differ, but also why their suitability varies under different urban conditions and whether these differences materially affect research conclusions.

This study approaches the problem from a GIScience perspective, focusing on the relationship between urban morphology, spatial representation, measurement uncertainty and inferential robustness.

## 3. Research Aim

The aim of this research is **to investigate how urban morphological characteristics influence the suitability of alternative spatial neighbourhood representations and to establish interpretable, evidence-based criteria for evaluating and selecting appropriate methods for urban spatial analysis.**

The research will address three questions:

**RQ1 — Method Suitability**

How does the suitability of alternative spatial neighbourhood representations vary across different urban morphological contexts and spatial scales?

**RQ2 — Mechanisms and Uncertainty Propagation**

Through what mechanisms do urban morphology and spatial representation choices interact to affect spatial measurements and statistical inference?

**RQ3 — Method Selection and Adaptability**

Can interpretable and transferable criteria be developed to guide the evaluation and selection of spatial neighbourhood representations across diverse and changing urban environments?

## 4. Literature Review

### 4.1 Spatial representation uncertainty: UGCoP and MAUP

The relationship between geographical representation and analytical outcomes has long been a fundamental concern in GIScience.

Fotheringham and Wong ([1991](https://doi.org/10.1068/a231025)) investigated the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) in multivariate statistical analysis, demonstrating that changes in spatial aggregation scale and zoning systems can alter parameter estimates and statistical conclusions. Their research established the importance of spatial unit specification in geographical modelling.

Kwan ([2012](https://doi.org/10.1080/00045608.2012.687349)) subsequently articulated the Uncertain Geographic Context Problem (UGCoP), emphasising that the geographical areas defined by researchers may not correspond to the contexts that actually influence the phenomena being studied. Unlike MAUP, which focuses on aggregation and zoning, UGCoP concerns uncertainty in the spatial and temporal boundaries of relevant geographical contexts.

Gotway and Young ([2002](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214502760047140)) further examined statistical challenges associated with combining spatial data collected at different scales and supports. Their work highlights how data alignment and spatial support can introduce additional uncertainty into analysis.

Together, these studies establish that geographical representations are not neutral technical decisions. However, they do not imply that one particular spatial representation is universally appropriate.

### 4.2 Urban morphology and geographical proximity

Urban morphology provides a potential basis for understanding why spatial analytical methods perform differently across urban environments.

Boeing ([2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s41109-019-0189-1)) examined street-network orientation, configuration and entropy, demonstrating how network structures can be quantitatively characterised. His large-scale study of [27,000 urban street networks](https://doi.org/10.1177/2399808318784595) also provided a methodological foundation for comparing connectivity, density and network organisation across urban areas.

Chen and Chen ([2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/19475683.2021.1966503)) investigated the relationship between network distance and straight-line distance across 25 Chinese cities. Their findings highlighted spatial variations in distance relationships and demonstrated the usefulness of the detour index for understanding potential distance-related bias.

Similarly, van Eldijk and colleagues ([2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.trd.2020.102410)) quantified the barrier effects of transport infrastructure on local accessibility, showing that motorways and railways can alter accessibility through changes in travel time, catchment areas and available route choices.

These studies demonstrate the importance of urban morphology and network structure in shaping accessibility. A further question is how such characteristics influence the analytical suitability of alternative neighbourhood representations.

### 4.3 Neighbourhood definitions and analytical sensitivity

Previous research has directly examined the consequences of different neighbourhood definitions.

James and colleagues ([2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2014.02.003)) compared different buffer sizes and shapes when measuring built-environment characteristics and examining their associations with walking and body mass index. Their findings demonstrated that neighbourhood delineation can influence both spatial measurements and statistical associations.

Li and colleagues ([2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12942-022-00310-7)) extended this discussion by comparing Euclidean and network buffers across six study sites. Through simulation and empirical analysis, they demonstrated that buffer choice could substantially alter estimated associations between built-environment characteristics and transport walking. Importantly, their simulation quantified bias under the assumption that network-based buffers better represented the relevant exposure.

These studies establish that representation choice can influence downstream statistical results. However, they also raise questions about why the magnitude of these consequences varies across geographical contexts and analytical settings.

### 4.4 Method selection and uncertainty evaluation

Recent methodological research has increasingly addressed the selection and adaptation of spatial representations.

Zare Sakhvidi and colleagues ([2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanplh.2025.101370)) proposed guidance for selecting buffers in greenspace–health research. Their framework emphasises research mechanisms, contextual characteristics and data considerations rather than arbitrary or universally preferred buffer distances.

Pais and colleagues ([2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijgi14050205)) examined public transport catchment areas under topographical constraints and explored simplified surrogate buffers as practical alternatives where detailed network analyses may not be feasible.

Comess and colleagues ([2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/jrsssc/qlag020)) developed spatially varying buffer radii using a hierarchical Bayesian approach, challenging the assumption that identical buffer sizes are appropriate throughout a study region.

Related approaches to uncertainty diagnostics already exist. Duque and colleagues ([2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0207377)) proposed S-maup to assess sensitivity to spatial aggregation, while Kedron and colleagues ([2022](https://doi.org/10.1080/03098265.2021.1901076)) demonstrated the application of specification curves for examining geographical uncertainty across alternative statistical model specifications.

These contributions indicate that sensitivity assessment and context-specific method selection are established research directions. Consequently, the novelty of the proposed study cannot rest solely on comparing buffers, measuring spatial differences or introducing a selection index.

### 4.5 Potential research gap

Taken together, the literature provides strong evidence that spatial representation choices can influence urban measurements and statistical results. It also offers established measures of network structure, accessibility barriers, aggregation sensitivity and methodological robustness.

Nevertheless, an area requiring further systematic investigation is **how urban morphological characteristics interact with alternative spatial representations to determine their task-specific analytical suitability**.

In particular, it remains worthwhile to examine when representation differences are amplified, attenuated or offset during the transition from spatial measurement to statistical inference, and whether the resulting relationships support interpretable and transferable method-selection criteria.

The proposed research will therefore focus on explaining the conditions and mechanisms governing method suitability, rather than simply demonstrating that alternative spatial methods produce different results.

This potential research gap will be further refined through detailed comparisons with existing studies on buffer sensitivity, spatial uncertainty diagnostics and context-dependent method selection.

## 5. Research Methods

The research will adopt a comparative and explanatory methodological design, combining GIS-based experiments, urban morphological analysis, Monte Carlo simulation and conventional spatial statistical methods. Pedestrian accessibility and urban built-environment measurement will provide the primary application context.

### 5.1 Study areas and data preparation

The initial study will select urban areas representing contrasting morphological characteristics, such as highly connected street networks, large-block developments and locations affected by physical barriers. Depending on data availability, two or three cities may be used for comparative validation, with sampling conducted across different neighbourhood types within each city.

Relevant datasets will include pedestrian-accessible road networks, buildings, land use, points of interest, water bodies, railways, pedestrian crossings and terrain information where appropriate. OpenStreetMap and available official datasets will be considered.

Data completeness, topological consistency, positional accuracy and temporal alignment will be assessed before analysis.

### 5.2 Urban morphological characterisation

Selected morphological characteristics will be quantified using interpretable indicators, including intersection density, street connectivity, block size, network circuity, crossing availability, barrier configuration and spatial heterogeneity of urban facilities.

These indicators will describe the conditions under which alternative spatial representations are evaluated.

### 5.3 Comparative spatial experiments

The first experimental stage will compare Euclidean buffers and pedestrian network-based catchments using equivalent distance budgets and multiple relevant scales, provisionally including 400 m, 800 m and 1,200 m.

Identical study locations, geographical datasets and measurement definitions will be maintained wherever possible.

Geometric differences will be quantified using area overlap, Jaccard similarity and differences in included spatial features. Selected built-environment measures, such as facility counts, facility density and land-use composition, will then be calculated under each representation.

This stage will examine how method sensitivity varies across urban morphological conditions.

### 5.4 Uncertainty propagation and statistical evaluation

The second stage will investigate whether differences in neighbourhood representation propagate into statistical estimates.

Where suitable independent outcome data are available, comparable statistical models will be estimated using variables derived from alternative representations. Differences in coefficient estimates, effect sizes, confidence intervals and model diagnostics will be examined.

Monte Carlo simulations will complement empirical analysis by introducing known spatial processes and model parameters. Controlled variations in network structure, spatial heterogeneity, neighbourhood definition and aggregation schemes will allow the study to examine the mechanisms responsible for amplification or attenuation of analytical differences.

Alternative data-generating scenarios will be considered to avoid assuming that one neighbourhood representation is universally correct.

Spatial autocorrelation, correlated estimates and uncertainty in the input data will be accounted for when assessing inferential robustness.

### 5.5 Developing and validating method-selection criteria

The final stage will synthesise the experimental evidence to identify conditions under which simpler representations are analytically adequate and circumstances in which more detailed methods are required.

Method suitability will be evaluated according to conceptual appropriateness, measurement sensitivity, inferential robustness, data requirements and computational costs.

Rather than relying on a universal numerical threshold, the study will explore task-specific criteria supported by uncertainty estimates and predefined tolerances for practically meaningful differences.

The transferability of these criteria will be examined across contrasting urban areas. Where reliable temporally comparable data are available, selected infrastructure-change scenarios may also be used to assess whether the criteria remain applicable as urban structures evolve.

## 6. Expected Results

The research is expected to generate three interconnected contributions.

First, it will provide a systematic empirical assessment of how alternative spatial neighbourhood representations behave across different urban morphological conditions. This will clarify the relationships between urban network structure, physical barriers, spatial scale and measurement sensitivity.

Second, the study will improve understanding of the mechanisms through which spatial representation uncertainty affects analytical results. By distinguishing geometric differences, measurement discrepancies and inferential consequences, the research aims to explain why some methodological choices lead to substantial changes in conclusions while others have limited effects.

Third, the study aims to develop an interpretable, context-adaptive framework for evaluating and selecting spatial neighbourhood representations. The framework could support researchers in deciding when simpler methods provide sufficiently robust results and when more detailed accessibility or sensitivity analyses are justified.

If stable selection criteria cannot be identified across all urban contexts, the study will instead clarify the limits of method transferability and identify the conditions under which further analysis is necessary.

Overall, the expected contribution is to advance GIScience from recognising spatial representation uncertainty towards understanding method suitability and supporting more transparent, reproducible and context-sensitive urban spatial analysis.
